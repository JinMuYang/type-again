The proposal to supply water cost-free to every household is often championed as a humanitarian necessity, grounded in the belief that access to water is a fundamental human right. While I acknowledge the moral intention behind this idea, I strongly disagree with its implementation. Providing water for free would inevitably lead to unsustainable wastefulness and ignore the significant economic and environmental costs of water processing.
Firstly, removing the financial cost of water would psychologically devalue it in the eyes of the consumer. It is a harsh reality of human psychology that people tend to take unconditional benefits for granted. If water were free, the immediate financial incentive to conserve it would vanish. This would lead to "mindless waste," such as leaving taps running or ignoring leaks, as there would be no personal monetary consequence. While a single household wasting water might seem negligible, the cumulative impact of millions of households behaving with such negligence would be catastrophic for total water reserves.
Furthermore, the argument for free water fails to account for the economic and logistical reality of its production. While it is true that over 70% of the Earth is covered in water, easily accessible freshwater constitutes a remarkably tiny fraction of this total. Whether it involves extracting and purifying groundwater, treating surface water from lakes and rivers, recycling municipal wastewater, or desalinating seawater, producing safe, potable water requires complex infrastructure. All of these processes are highly capital-intensive and energy-demanding. If the end-user does not pay for this, the financial burden falls entirely on the state, draining public funds that could otherwise be allocated to essential services like healthcare or education. Essentially, every liter of water wasted represents not just a loss of the liquid itself, but a squandering of the energy and resources required to treat and distribute it.
Finally, we must consider the ethical implications of resource management on a global and intergenerational scale. We live on a planet with finite freshwater resources. In a "horizontal" sense, squandering water in affluent regions deepens the disparity with drought-stricken areas where water is "liquid gold." In a "vertical" sense, our current usage dictates what we leave for future generations. A policy of free water promotes a culture of excess that threatens to leave our descendants with depleted reservoirs and a compromised environment.
In conclusion, while the desire to provide free water is born of compassion, it is practically unsound. To ensure a sustainable future, water must remain a priced commodity. This price serves not only to cover the costs of modern sanitation and distribution but, more importantly, to act as a constant reminder of the resource's precious and finite nature.

The duration of school summer holidays, which often spans two months or more, is a subject of ongoing debate. While some argue that such long breaks are inefficient, others maintain that they are essential for a child's holistic development.
Supporters of extended holidays emphasise the necessity of unstructured free time. By being liberated from the rigid constraints of the compulsory curriculum, students are given the space to decompress. Although national education systems are meticulously designed, they often function as a "one-size-fits-all" model. A long break allows children to step outside this common denominator and explore their individual interests and fortes. For instance, two months is sufficient time for a child to master a new skill, such as a programming language, or to address specific academic weaknesses at their own pace. Furthermore, these breaks offer a rare opportunity to step away from the classroom and engage with the wider world, be it through physical activity in nature or family adventures.
Conversely, critics argue that the summer vacation should be shortened. They contend that childhood is the most neuroplastic and adaptive phase of human life; therefore, letting months pass without structured learning is a wasted opportunity. Moreover, freedom does not always lead to positive outcomes. A two-month hiatus provides ample opportunity for negative habits to take root, often with greater ease than disciplined positive ones. The "summer slide"—characterised by a disrupted biological clock and a shortened attention span—often reverberates long into the new term. In worst-case scenarios, a lack of supervision can lead to excessive screen time or even substance abuse, which inevitably undermines future academic performance.
In my view, the long summer holiday is, in itself, a neutral policy. Its impact—whether beneficial or detrimental—depends entirely on how the time is utilised. While the risks of idleness are real, the opportunity for self-discovery and mental restitution is too valuable to lose. Ultimately, the responsibility lies not with the school schedule, but with parents and guardians to ensure this substantial amount of time is managed effectively.

The view that we should avoid unnecessary air travel is gaining traction among those concerned with planetary health. I firmly agree with this stance; while reducing air travel may cause individual inconvenience, the long-term environmental benefits unequivocally outweigh these temporary drawbacks.
It is often argued that air travel is indispensable in the modern era, yet this overlooks the viability of current ground-based alternatives. While aviation is a relatively recent phenomenon in human history, our fundamental need to migrate and explore has always found a way. Today, the infrastructure for alternative transport is more robust than ever. High-speed rail networks span vast landmasses, and highway systems connect even the most remote areas, making cars and buses feasible options. Therefore, choosing to fly is often a matter of preference for speed and luxury rather than an absolute necessity. Although overland travel requires a greater investment of time, the trade-off is often a richer, more grounded travel experience that aviation cannot provide.
From an environmental perspective, the argument for limiting flights is compelling. Aviation is a major contributor to greenhouse gas emissions, particularly carbon dioxide, which accelerates global warming. While a single cancelled flight may seem negligible in isolation, the cumulative impact of a collective shift in behaviour would be profound. Reducing our reliance on planes can significantly mitigate the domino effects of climate change—such as glacier retreat, permafrost thawing, and the destabilisation of ecosystems. By making these sacrifices, we directly contribute to preserving a habitable biosphere for humans and countless other species.
Ultimately, the speed at which we travel is irrelevant if the destination itself is compromised. There is no sanctuary from a degrading atmosphere. The inconvenience of taking a train instead of a plane pales in comparison to the existential threat of climate collapse. It is imperative that we prioritise eco-friendly transport; if these individual choices propagate on a global scale, humanity can steer the Earth toward a sustainable and secure future.


The diagram illustrates the multi-stage process of manufacturing fabric from bamboo plants.
Overall, the production is a linear process comprising nine distinct steps. It begins with the agricultural cultivation of the raw material and progresses through various mechanical and chemical treatments to produce finished garments.
The initial phase involves farming. Bamboo is planted during the spring season and, once matured, is harvested in the autumn. Following harvest, the whole plants are cut into strips by a mechanical saw. These strips are then crushed to form a liquid pulp.
The subsequent phase focuses on refining the material. The pulp undergoes a filtration process to separate the long fibres from the liquid content. To ensure the fibres are pliable, they are softened by adding a mixture of water and amine oxide. Once treated, the fibres are spun into yarn, which is then woven to create fabric. Finally, this fabric is utilised to manufacture clothing items, such as T-shirts and socks.

In contemporary society, global fashion trends heavily dictate how people dress, largely transcending geographical boundaries. This phenomenon is primarily driven by the intrinsic human desire for aesthetic appeal, coupled with the ubiquitous reach of modern social media. However, I contend that this homogenization of fashion is a definitively negative development, carrying damaging practical and psychological consequences.
The root of this trend lies in human nature and technological advancement. Beauty is inherently a laudatory concept, and it is entirely natural for individuals to appreciate and imitate aesthetically pleasing styles. However, with the advent of the internet, global fashion trends can now disseminate across the world with unprecedented ease. Once a celebrity posts photographs sporting designer garments on social media, that stylish look is rapidly emulated by their followers. This is further amplified by sophisticated algorithms designed by tech conglomerates, which push these trends onto the screens of susceptible audiences, blinding them to the reality that a curated online aesthetic does not equate to a meaningful life.
While drawing inspiration from fashion influencers is not inherently wrong, excessive conformity is highly detrimental to the average person. Garments endorsed by celebrities often cost thousands of dollars, placing them firmly in the luxury category, with retail prices far exceeding their actual production costs. If an ordinary, working-class individual resolves to purchase such prohibitively expensive clothing, they are usually forced to compromise on more essential aspects of their livelihood. Ultimately, the dopamine-induced pleasure of acquiring these items is fleeting, leaving behind only financial regret and little substantive benefit. Furthermore, even if the emulated outfits are affordable, constantly basing one’s aesthetics on the choices of others inevitably erodes self-identity. Just as conformity often stems from a fear of disapproval, relentlessly chasing fashion trends typically reflects an underlying psychological vulnerability: an over-reliance on external validation.
In conclusion, while a primal love for beauty and modern telecommunications have cemented global fashion's role in daily life, this is ultimately a negative trend. Because a healthy role model entails much more than superficial appearance, conformism in the realm of exorbitant fashion fosters financial instability and adverse psychological issues. It leaves individuals treating their daily lives as a perpetual form of cosplay rather than cultivating their authentic selves.
