Whether tall apartment blocks are the best solution to a housing shortage really depends on which half of the problem is being solved: the physical one, or the human one. I agree that building upward is close to the only realistic way to house a growing city once land runs out — but I do not agree that height alone produces "enough" homes, because supply and access are not the same thing.
On the physical side, height is hard to avoid. Cities concentrate the things that pull people away from the countryside — reliable infrastructure, dense job markets, specialised healthcare and education — faster than any city can expand outward to fit them. Hong Kong shows both the necessity and the payoff of building up rather than out: hemmed in by hills and harbour, it has housed millions of people on a few hundred square kilometres of usable land, something a low-rise model could never have achieved. Wherever horizontal growth is geographically capped, height stops being one option among several and becomes close to the only one.
The human side is a different matter. A tower of a thousand flats provides no housing at all to the people priced out of it, and it takes surprisingly little for supply to be absorbed by demand that has nothing to do with actually living there. Vancouver is a useful case: a wave of investment buying left a visible share of new high-rise units sitting empty while long-term residents struggled to find anywhere to rent, and the city eventually introduced a tax specifically targeting unoccupied homes. Height had solved the engineering problem; on its own, it had done nothing for the residents the housing was supposedly built for.
Tall buildings, then, are a necessary foundation rather than a complete solution. A city that relies on height alone risks ending up with an impressive skyline sitting on top of a persistent housing crisis. What actually turns available space into homes people can live in is height paired with deliberate policy — vacancy taxes, limits on speculative ownership, or guaranteed quotas of public housing. The apartment blocks solve the arithmetic; the policy decides whom the answer includes.

There is no unanimity on whether theatres and cinemas still matter in the age of streaming. This essay contends that both views hold genuine merit: online platforms have transformed how people access entertainment, yet theatres and cinemas retain economic and cultural value that digital alternatives cannot fully replace. In my view, streaming and physical venues complement one another, and the choice between them ultimately comes down to context and personal preference.
Proponents of the first view point to the meteoric rise of streaming giants such as Netflix and HBO, now among the fastest-growing businesses in the world. From decades-old film noir to the latest prestige series, viewers can find almost anything in a single app, often for a fraction of the price of a cinema ticket, and a large 4K television with surround sound now rivals the multiplex. Even the stage is within easy reach: the filmed version of Hamilton, released on Disney+, brought a Broadway hit into living rooms far from New York.
Others maintain that theatres and cinemas remain economically and culturally indispensable. Economically, both support a wide web of employment, from projectionists and snack-bar staff to actors and stagehands. Cinemas often sit alongside shopping centres and restaurants, boosting local footfall, while theatre districts such as London's West End draw millions of theatregoers whose spending sustains nearby hotels and cafés. Culturally, theatres preserve and renew a living tradition of performance, from Shakespeare to new writing, while cinema is celebrated at the Cannes, Venice and Berlin film festivals, the so-called "Big Three", which draw professionals and enthusiasts by the thousands every year.
Having considered both sides, I do not believe that a choice has to be made, because each form offers something the others cannot. Theatres provide the unrepeatable immediacy of live performance, with actors and audience sharing the same room and the same moment. Cinemas offer an immersive, communal experience that no home setup can replicate, and remain the first place to see major releases. Streaming, meanwhile, offers total flexibility and carries the stage to audiences who could never make the trip to a theatre district. It also hosts the many independent and niche productions that never receive a theatrical release, whether for budgetary reasons or simply a lack of screens. The three are, in other words, not rivals so much as parts of one ecosystem, each filling a gap the others leave open.
In conclusion, this is not a debate with a single winner, and it may not need one. Some will always prefer to watch films at home, others will keep seeking out the cinema or the stage for their scale and sense of occasion, and many, of course, have little interest in film or theatre at all, which is just as valid a position. Small screen or big screen, a film is a film.

The diagram illustrates the sequence of atmospheric and geographical events that lead to the formation of a rain-shadow desert, an arid region that develops inland from a coastal mountain range.
Overall, the process comprises seven linked stages and centres on a single contrast: air arriving from the sea deposits almost all of its moisture as rain on the mountains' windward slope, so that by the time it descends the leeward side, it is left almost entirely dry. It is this persistent aridity that, over time and often thousands of kilometres inland, gives rise to the rain-shadow desert.
The process begins as prevailing winds move in off the sea and reach the coast. On meeting the mountain range, they are forced upwards, and as this moist air rises, it steadily cools. The falling temperature causes clouds to form on the windward slope, and rain begins to fall from them even before the air mass reaches the summit.
Having released the bulk of its moisture on the seaward side, the air that crosses the mountain peak onto the leeward slope is now comparatively dry. It continues to descend and travels onward across the inland plains beyond the foot of the mountains, carrying little to no further rainfall with it. As a result, these inland areas receive negligible precipitation, and it is this prolonged dryness that eventually produces the arid, rain-shadow desert shown in the diagram.

Universities in many countries already send students abroad or place them with employers for part of their degree, and I would argue that making this compulsory across all undergraduate programmes is a change worth embracing. Although the financial cost and the emotional strain of adjusting to an unfamiliar environment are real concerns, they are outweighed by the practical skills, cross-cultural insight and professional connections that such experiences provide.
The drawbacks are not trivial. A semester overseas routinely costs several times more than a semester at home once airfare, accommodation and living expenses are added to tuition — a burden that low-income students and their families may simply be unable to absorb without a scholarship. Work placements bring a different kind of difficulty: a marketing student parachuted into a real agency, for example, may discover that the easy-going rhythm of campus life gives way to tight deadlines, demanding clients and office politics that no lecture ever prepared them for. Some students, particularly those who have not yet developed thick skin, find this transition genuinely disheartening.
Even so, the professional gains from a placement are hard to overstate. Coursework can simulate a task, but it cannot replicate the accountability of knowing that a mistake will cost a real client real money. A computer-science student who spends six months debugging production code at a software firm learns habits that no seminar can teach — writing for other engineers to read, working within someone else's deadlines, defending a decision to a sceptical manager. Employers benefit too: many placement students are quietly earmarked for graduate roles, which gives companies a low-risk way of securing talent before competitors even see it on the market.
Time spent studying abroad pays similar dividends in a different currency. The EU's Erasmus+ programme, which has sent several million students to study in another European country since it began, is frequently cited by participants as the moment they first became genuinely fluent in a second language, precisely because there was no English-speaking classmate to fall back on when the local language got difficult. Beyond language, everyday life in an unfamiliar culture — adjusting to different norms around punctuality, hierarchy or classroom debate — reshapes how students see their own assumptions, an outcome that reading about other cultures rarely achieves. The friendships formed in the process often turn into professional networks that span borders, an asset in almost any career built after graduation.
Weighed against each other, the two sides of this argument are not evenly matched. The costs of a placement or a period abroad are real but temporary and, in many cases, can be offset by scholarships, paid placements or part-time work; the benefits, by contrast, compound over an entire career in the form of sharper skills, broader perspective and a wider professional network. For this reason, I remain convinced that requiring every undergraduate to spend time studying abroad or on placement would leave graduates — and the employers who eventually hire them — better off.

Concern that primary schools lean too heavily on formal instruction is understandable in today's exam-driven culture, and I share it in part: play is far more valuable than many classrooms acknowledge. However, I do not believe formal teaching itself is the problem. It should remain the foundation of primary education, with purposeful play as an essential partner rather than a replacement.
The case for play begins with children's wellbeing. When a doctrine of valuing exam results above all else permeates a school, pupils can become anxious and exhausted at an age when they should be discovering a love of learning. Adults struggle to concentrate under unbroken strain, let alone children, so a short, playful activity that lets attention recover is not a distraction from learning but a means of sustaining it. Indeed, the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child lists play among the rights of every child. Play is also a powerful way to teach. A vocabulary game played in teams builds word knowledge and cooperation at once, and using building blocks to model fractions lets pupils see and handle an abstract idea in a way that a worksheet alone rarely can.
Play, however, complements formal instruction; it cannot replace it. Literacy, numeracy and scientific reasoning are rarely acquired through unguided exploration alone; they depend on deliberate, sequenced teaching, which matters most for children who receive little academic support at home. Weaken that structure, and educational inequality is likely to widen, since better-resourced families can simply compensate with private tuition. Formal learning also instils the discipline and sustained attention on which later, more demanding study depends; since habits formed in early childhood tend to endure, the primary years are the time to build them. Equally, play must have a purpose: an activity that amuses pupils without teaching them anything is merely lost instruction time. The real target of criticism, then, is the distortion of formal learning into relentless test preparation, not its place in the curriculum.
In conclusion, I agree only in part that primary schools place too much emphasis on formal learning. Structured teaching must remain the foundation, particularly for pupils with little support at home; what needs to change is the status of play, which should be treated as an essential and purposeful part of the school day rather than a reward for finished work. A classroom that treats formal teaching and play as partners rather than rivals is far better placed to educate the whole child.
